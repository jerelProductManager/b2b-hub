B2B Hub Modernization · Competitive Research

What CDW, Grainger, Amazon Business and PBteen Do That We Don't

A full-funnel teardown of four portals our buyers already know — anonymous browsing, account sign-up and verification, and the logged-in account layer — scored against the gaps named in PRD v1.2 and the Logged-In Experience memo. Three are pure-play B2B distributors. The fourth is a consumer retailer with a business program bolted on, which is exactly our situation.

The short version

Four different bets on what a B2B portal is for

None of the four is uniformly better than the others, and each is beatable in a place that matters to us. Read the weakness line as our opening, not their flaw.

CDW

Rep-led. The portal supports the human.

Nearly every CTA on a segment page is “Contact an Expert” or a phone number. The digital account — branded Rubi — is positioned as a supplement to a named account manager, not a replacement for one. Contract pages are unusually generous with detail and with people's names.

Moat
Contract-page depth and per-contract human routing
Weak
No searchable contract finder anywhere; commercial segment pages look unmaintained
Steal
Free approval workflows as the account hook

Grainger

Operations-led. The account is the product.

The storefront is ordinary; the account configuration machinery is not. Order approval routing, per-approver limits, locked payment and shipping fields, and up to eleven custom order fields that print onto packing slips and invoices — that is the real switching cost, because it makes Grainger's paperwork reconcile inside the customer's ERP.

Moat
Contract affiliation funnel + ERP-shaped order data
Weak
No tax-exempt self-service, no spend dashboard, punchout names zero platforms publicly
Steal
Per-state contract pages and the three-way registration fork

Amazon Business

Self-service-led. Friction is the enemy.

Registration is an email, a company name and a self-declared role. Verification is an exception path, not a gate. Complexity is hidden by tier and seat count rather than by a wizard, so a two-person account structurally cannot encounter the enterprise surface.

Moat
Zero-friction entry, then policy tooling that compounds
Weak
No onboarding checklist at all; contract linkage is still a 3-day human callback
Steal
Domain-level account claiming; SLAs published beside every path

PBteen / WSI

Program-led. There is no B2B storefront.

PBteen has no B2B program of its own — nor does Pottery Barn or West Elm. There is one program, WSI B2B, on its own domain, and each consumer brand hosts a thin funnel page into it. Approved members shop the ordinary consumer site with a discount flag on their account. Our closest structural analog by far, and the only one of the four that has solved the consumer-to-business identity problem.

Moat
One membership across ten brands, and a published discount that beats the public sale
Weak
No quoting, no roles, no approvals, no punchout, no analytics — nothing behind the login
Steal
The Member ID bridge onto an existing consumer account

Flow teardown

The specific moves worth copying

Ordered the way a buyer meets them: land, qualify, sign up, operate. Each item is an interaction we can specify, not a general impression.

Stage 01

Landing & contract discovery

Grainger

A state selector that fans out to 51 contract pages. /content/state-local carries a dropdown for all fifty states plus D.C., each routing to a dedicated page. California's page names the vehicle, the number 7-25-51-02, the term Jan 1 2025 – Aug 31 2026, who is eligible, a link into caleprocure.ca.gov, and two named humans — the state's own contract administrator and Grainger's government sales manager, each with a direct line.

The cautionary half: /content/state-of-texas returns a 404 while still sitting in the live dropdown. If we build this template in CMS Light, publishing needs a completeness check before a state can appear in the picker.

Grainger

A numbered Affiliate → Register → Shop strip on every contract page. Three visible steps turn an opaque procurement process into an obvious next action — and it directly addresses our Contracts-page misuse problem, where customers who already hold a contract land confused and generate misdirected support contacts.

CDW

“Shop by Public Contract” sits above the fold on every public-sector segment page — higher than solution content. A procurement officer's first question is whether they can buy on their vehicle at all; CDW answers it before the scroll.

CDW also publishes what most sellers hide: the SEWP V page states its 0.34% order surcharge openly, and the OMNIA storefront surfaces an Apple products restriction in the top third of the page, before browsing. Killing a bad path early beats a failed checkout.

CDW

One dedicated email alias and one named program manager per contract — eandi@cdwg.com, sourcewellcontract@cdwg.com, GSAPM@Siriusfederal.com. Inbound mail arrives pre-routed and pre-qualified. The GSA page goes further and lists the government's own contracting officer alongside CDW's staff, which is a trust move we could make cheaply.

Amazon

Two-axis information architecture: who you are × what you buy. /industries/{vertical} runs in parallel with /supplies/{category}, so a buyer can arrive by identity or by need. Within a vertical, sub-segments are tabs — K-12 and Higher Ed share one Education page rather than splitting into two. With twelve segments to maintain in CMS Light, that consolidation matters.

Both CTAs are offered side by side throughout: Contact sales next to Create a free account. Neither audience is forced down the other's funnel.

PBteen / WSI

The discount is published, flat, and guaranteed to beat the public sale. 20% off at eight brands, 25% at Williams Sonoma Home, no minimum purchase — and then the rule that matters: if the brand is running a promotion that equals or exceeds the membership discount, the member gets an additional 5% off.

That single clause answers the objection every consumer retailer's B2B program faces, and the one we will hear constantly: your Black Friday price is better than my business price, so why did I sign up? Volume pricing stays unpublished and routed through a rep — a published floor with a negotiated ceiling.

PBteen / WSI

The B2B assortment is merchandised inside the consumer catalog. westelm.com/shop/business-contract-grade is a shoppable category on the consumer site — Seating, Case Goods, Contract Bath, Office & Workspace — with a Learn More About Contract Grade explainer sitting inside it. A shopper browsing chairs can discover the program from the product rather than from the nav, and it costs no navigation real estate. B2B appears in the footer and in the signed-out account menu, never in the top nav.

The gap they leave open: a consumer buying twelve identical chairs gets no prompt at all. No quantity trigger, no category nudge, no this looks like a business order. For us that is nearly free to build and would beat them outright.

PBteen / WSI

One membership, many service tracks. Trade, Contract, Business Solutions and Corporate Gifting are four service models behind a single application — and Trade versus Contract is defined by end use, not customer type: residential single-residence purchases versus public or commercial space needing contract-grade product. Segmenting the service rather than the program is worth considering against our twelve separate segment pages.

The cautionary note is content governance. Mark & Graham still publishes a retired tiered discount schedule pointing at a dead domain and instructing applicants to fax their credentials; brand counts contradict across pages (8, 9 and 10); footers carry both To The Trade and B2B Program as separate links. This is precisely the drift CMS Light exists to prevent.

Stage 02

Sign-up & verification

Amazon

Verification is an exception path, not a gate. Registration takes a work email, a company name, a name and password, and a self-declared role. The verification line reads: If we require additional information to verify your business, we'll let you know. Documents — EIN, business license, sealed state documents — are framed as to speed up verification, which converts a blocker into a choice the user controls.

This is the sharpest divergence from our model. Our highest-volume post-landing support trigger is a failed D&B check at the door. Amazon's answer to bad verification error messaging is to take verification out of the critical path entirely. Worth noting: I found no evidence Amazon uses D&B for buyer accounts at all.

Amazon

Publish the rejection list before submission. The tax-exemption flow states plainly that sales tax permits and W9s are not accepted, names the edge cases it does handle (tribal IDs, diplomat cards), and admits its limitation (no partial exemptions). Preventing the failure is cheaper than writing a better failure message — and it is directly portable to our three verification paths.

It also offers two routes with honest, published SLAs side by side: upload a form → up to 24 hours or self-guided wizard → active in 15 minutes. The user trades effort for speed using real numbers instead of waiting blind.

Grainger

The three-way fork is the first screen, before any fields. Register and join an existing business account / Register and create a new business account / Register as a personal user. One click resolves the hardest question in B2B onboarding, and the Grainger account number is optional rather than required.

Amazon

The consumer-account fork is phrased as a shopping question, not an account-architecture question. Users choose between organization business shopping only and a mix of organization business and personal shopping, and the consequence is stated at the decision point — order history merges, or the personal account keeps its history and its Prime benefits.

Then Account Authority inverts the problem entirely. The organization verifies its email domain via a DNS record (Unverified → Verified, typically 1–2 days), after which the system routes new account creation on that domain through the org's approval, detects employees who started a consumer account on the domain, and prompts them to switch. For a retailer whose B2B buyers overwhelmingly already have personal accounts with us, this is the structurally correct fix.

All three

Nobody has an onboarding checklist. Not CDW, not Grainger, not Amazon, not WSI — Amazon's Getting started page is a modular marketing landing page with no numbered sequence, no checklist, no progress bar. Amazon substitutes setup guides forked by org size (under ten users with no groups gets a “Simple” guide that routes larger orgs elsewhere) and role-filtered learning content. The Phase 2 onboarding checklist in our memo is genuinely uncontested ground.

PBteen / WSI

The business account is an entitlement layered onto the consumer account, not a second login. At {brand}.com/trade-signin/login.html the page says Connect your B2B membership — sign in with your 12-digit B2B Member ID and Access Code to begin. It is a one-time connection, and it is explicitly built for three states: a member who hasn't connected yet, a member who signed up in a physical store, and a member recently added to an existing company account.

This is the pattern that fits us best of anything in this deck. We have retail stores, a consumer account base that most of our B2B buyers already sit inside, and a My Account platform in flight. One identity with a business flag on top beats asking a customer to keep two accounts.

PBteen / WSI

Qualification is document review, not an entity lookup — and it accepts individual credentials. Applicants supply any two qualifying documents from a published, profession-specific list: a business website, business license, articles of organization, EIN letter, reseller's certificate — and for designers, trade-body membership (ASID, IIDA, NCIDQ) or a design degree. Application takes 5–8 minutes; review takes 48–72 business hours.

A freelance designer with a portfolio site and an NCIDQ passes here and fails a D&B lookup. That is the right trade for a discount program with no credit attached, and the wrong one if net terms are on the table — worth deciding which risk we are actually carrying before we tune our own gate. The mechanic to copy regardless: benefits unlock when the confirmation email lands, not when the physical card arrives four weeks later. No dead zone.

Tax exemption is decoupled from approval and handled per state with six exemption types — and, importantly, only one set of documents is required per firm. Firm-level, not per-user.

Stage 03

The logged-in account

CDW

Approval workflows are given away free, as the hook. The Purchase Authorization System is explicitly a complimentary workflow tool: unlimited workflows, six rule types, unlimited approvers per rule, drag-and-drop rule ordering, purchasers grouped by department or location, 1-day implementation. CDW then ladders heavier needs into paid eProcurement.

The detail that shows they have actually operated this: approvers stay active during out-of-office, and approver lists update in real time as team composition changes. That is the failure mode that kills most B2B approval features, and CDW markets the fix.

CDW

Rubi's navigation is organized by intent, not by data object — See It All In One Place / Take Action / Get Updates and Advice, rather than Orders / Invoices / Users / Settings. Underneath, the account splits Company Favorites from Personal Favorites, and Order Reporting from Payment Reporting. Software keycodes and downloads get top-level placement rather than being buried in order detail.

Account Team is itself a persistent logged-in page with its own URL — the rep relationship modeled as a durable object rather than a phone number in the footer. Given how much of our B2B business runs through named reps, that is close to free and worth a lot.

Grainger

Three limit types, not one: order-level thresholds, cumulative time-period spend (month / quarter / year), and consumable budget allocations. Limits are set per approver with escalation upward, and notifications fire to every role by email, on-site, and mobile. Setup offers Express or Custom — a fast default for the impatient and full configuration for the sophisticated.

Grainger

Custom order fields that print onto the paperwork. Up to three line-item fields (part number, PO line, notes) and up to eight order-level fields, each free-text or dropdown, required or optional — flowing through to packing slips and invoices. Amazon's equivalent goes further: 19 fields, 13 custom, up to 500 dropdown values, feeding order documents, cXML e-invoices, analytics and approval routing rules.

This is the deepest switching cost either company has, because it makes their documents reconcile inside the customer's ERP without manual keying.

Grainger

“Previously Purchased” is a search facet. It requires sign-in, which makes signing in immediately worth something, and it turns account history into a merchandising surface. Alongside it sit facets keyed to buyer mandates rather than product attributes: Country of Origin, Registered SBA small business, Environmentally Preferable, and stock filtered by chosen branch.

Those compliance facets map straight onto Federal, Diversity Suppliers and State/Local — three of our twelve segments — and connect to the diversity spend report already sketched for Phase 3.

Amazon

Policies explain themselves at the moment of friction. Guided Buying has three severities — preferred items get a badge and their own section in search results; restricted items carry a warning or force approval; blocked items cannot be bought. The admin writes a custom message that appears on the product page in the organization's own voice, so the rule teaches rather than merely blocks.

Compare our portal exclusions today, which are invisible to the buyer until a category page returns nothing — the dead-category bug from the Shia Bernstein interview is the same problem viewed from the other side.

Amazon

Spend Visibility is a real dashboard, powered by QuickSight, with Supplier Diversity, Spend by Category, Compliance and Spend Details views, and its own three-role model (Administrator / Author / Reader) separate from account roles. Underneath it, a free reconciliation report that expanded from 34 to 75 columns at line-item level.

Grainger, notably, has no spend dashboard — its reporting is export-to-CSV. Between those two, the bar for our Phase 2 spend analytics is Amazon, not Grainger.

Amazon

Punchout configures itself. Business Settings → System integrations → Configure purchasing system, a self-configuration tool, a published punchout guide, and — the part worth stealing — a Test Mode for validating the connection before going live. Both CDW and Grainger route integration entirely through a rep.

PBteen / WSI

A 60-minute appointment is the hinge between self-serve and a human. Bookable in three modalities — on-location (we can come to you), in store, or virtual — with a published agenda: inventory availability, swatches, project management help. Underneath it sits a Find Your Account Executive state dropdown covering every US state and Canadian province, with reps split by architecture/design versus owner/developer. For anyone who can't tell which appointment they need, there's a chat fallback.

It converts anonymous intent into a named relationship without a phone tree, which is a more realistic model for our consultative segments than expecting a Federal buyer to self-serve their way through a portal.

PBteen / WSI

And then there is nothing behind the login. No quote builder — every quote is an email to contractsales@wsgc.com. No multi-user accounts, roles, spend limits, approval workflows, cost centers, PO capture, punchout or ERP integration. No spend reporting. Order history shows Total Projects (0) / Total Orders (0) and warns that new orders may take 24 hours to appear.

Worth sitting with, because WSI is the closest structural match to us in this set: a consumer retailer running a business program off its consumer storefront. They solved the front of the funnel — identity, discount clarity, merchandised assortment, service routing — and built none of the procurement layer. If we only do the same, we will win designers and lose procurement officers.

All four

None of them notifies a customer that a contract is expiring. All four publish dated commitments — Grainger's California vehicle expires Aug 31 2026, its UCOP vehicle already lapsed, WSI memberships run two years and then need renewing — and none has any documented proactive alert about expiry, new eligibility, or pricing changes. Notification effort goes to shipments, approvals, back-in-stock and expiring payment cards.

That is the whitespace. It is also exactly the item the Logged-In memo already identifies as the strongest driver of return visits.

Exposure

Where we're behind, and where they are

Our exposed flanks

Verification blocks at the door

Amazon lets a buyer transact before verification finishes; WSI unlocks benefits the moment the confirmation email lands. We stop them at D&B and then explain it poorly. This is a model problem, not a copy problem.

Our consumer and business accounts don't talk

Most of our B2B buyers already have a bhphotovideo.com account and many enroll through the store. WSI solved this with a one-time Member ID connection; we still treat the two identities as unrelated.

Contracts and procurement end in a mailto

Grainger runs a distinct affiliation form per vehicle with a 48-hour SLA. Amazon publishes a 3-business-day advisor callback. We publish an email address.

No logged-in reason to return

Once a customer has a punchout portal, we have nothing left to show them. Every competitor has an account layer that keeps earning the visit.

Governance tooling is absent

Roles, approvals and spend limits are table stakes at all three. CDW gives them away free specifically to lock in the buying organization's process.

Portal rules are invisible to the buyer

Our ~350 portal parameters silently shape what a customer can see and buy, with no explanation surfaced anywhere. Amazon turns the same rules into labeled, self-explaining signposts.

Their exposed flanks

Nobody warns about contract expiry

Zero of four, despite all four publishing dated commitments. Our single strongest planned feature has no competitor to beat.

Nobody has an onboarding checklist

Amazon's getting-started page is marketing copy. A real progress indicator for newly approved accounts would be genuinely novel here.

Nobody detects business intent in a consumer cart

WSI is the only one of the four with a consumer storefront to work with, and it does nothing: someone buying twelve identical chairs gets no prompt. A quantity or category trigger is cheap and we have the same storefront.

WSI has no procurement layer at all

No quotes, roles, approvals, spend limits, PO capture, punchout or analytics. The retailer-run B2B program closest to ours has built nothing a procurement officer needs.

CDW has no contract finder

Its federal contracts page is a static card list — no search, no filter, no state or entity-type facet. A filterable finder beats the market leader outright.

Grainger hides its punchout capability

It names no platform publicly — not Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, SAP or Oracle — and offers no self-service intake. Publishing our spec wins the technical evaluation by default.

Grainger has no tax-exempt self-service

No upload flow, no help article, no account surface — only a generic contact form. Disproportionately painful for government, education and nonprofit buyers.

Integration health is nobody's feature

Amazon offers a pre-launch test mode; none of the three shows a live customer whether their punchout is currently working. A broken punchout is a silent blocker today.

Recommendations

What to adopt, in order

Ranked by impact against the gaps PRD v1.2 already names, weighted toward items that reduce support volume. Phase tags use our own P0–P3 scale, not the competitor's roadmap.

01

Move verification off the critical path

Let a qualifying account register and browse immediately, and run D&B, domain whitelist or manual review as an exception that gates checkout or net terms — not the door. Frame documents as acceleration (to speed this up, have your EIN ready) rather than as a requirement.

This reframes our largest support driver from an error-message project into a flow-design decision, and it is the one change here that all three competitors would recognize as obvious.

02

Publish the rejection list and the SLA before submission

For each of the three verification paths, state what is accepted, what is explicitly not accepted, and how long each route takes — side by side, with real numbers. Amazon's 15 minutes vs. up to 24 hours pairing lets the buyer choose their own trade-off; our CMS-editable error copy per error code should be paired with CMS-editable pre-submission guidance.

It also gives Pattern A and Pattern B somewhere to converge: the fork is compatible with both segment-first and qualification-first, so it can ship ahead of the research decision.

WSI's version is the single most transferable pattern in this deck, because their situation is ours — a consumer retailer with physical stores, an existing account base, and a business program running on the same storefront. Deliberately not adopting

Patterns to leave alone

CDW

The two-domain split

CDW runs cdw.com and cdwg.com as parallel sites with separate chrome. It fragments maintenance and SEO, and their commercial segment pages have visibly gone stale as a result. Twelve segments inside one CMS-managed hub is the right structure for us.

Grainger

Rep-gating the high-intent paths

eProcurement, KeepStock, tax exemption and net terms all terminate in “contact your rep.” It protects the relationship and loses the evaluation. Every path we can make self-service, we should.

Grainger

Two competing URL schemes

A modern /product-collections/industries tree coexists with legacy flat pages carrying inconsistent casing and underscores. A cautionary tale for our own CMS migration: retire the old paths, don't run both.

Amazon

Gating analytics behind a paid tier

Prime Business tiers spend visibility and blocking policies behind $179–$10,099 a year. We have no subscription motion and no reason to build one; our tiering should follow account size, not payment.

Grainger

Onsite inventory programs

KeepStock's vending machines and Grainger-managed stockrooms are a consumables play. Photo, video and pro audio capital equipment does not replenish that way — the transferable half is the reorder list, not the hardware.

All four

Pages that outlive their programs

Grainger's UCOP vehicle expired in June and its page still stands behind a state dropdown that 404s. Mark & Graham still publishes a retired tiered discount, a dead domain, and a fax number. Whatever we build needs an expiry-aware publish state, or CMS Light will accumulate the same drift we are trying to escape.

WSI

Client-side-rendered acquisition pages

The PBteen B2B page returns navigation, promo banners and a footer to any client that doesn't execute JavaScript — the entire B2B pitch is invisible to crawlers.
